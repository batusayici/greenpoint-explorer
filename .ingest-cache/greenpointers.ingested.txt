content.rendered: This September, a new neighborhood bar called Susu’s is opening at 229 Kent Avenue in Williamsburg, in the former home of Grand Ferry , a tavern that closed in 2018. Susu’s will be an easy-going spot that serves cocktails, natural wine, and bar food. Susu’s calls itself “a bar made by bartenders.” The owners are Steven Bishop and his brother Michael Bishop-Myers, who was the general manager of Pencil Factory (142 Franklin St.), the beloved local bar that closed last summer. Several Pencil Factory staff members followed Bishop-Myers and are part of the opening team at Susu’s. The owners said that the idea behind Susu’s is that customers shouldn’t have to fight for a reservation and that getting a good drink shouldn’t be difficult. “I’ve been bartending in New York for basically a decade. I just wanted to build the bar my friends and I actually want to drink in,” said owner Michael Bishop-Myers in a press release. “No host, no reservations, you walk in and I make you a drink.” The press release calls Susu’s “easygoing like a corner dive,” but “with food and drinks that belong somewhere much fancier.” The kitchen is helmed by Chef Garrett Kawabata, a former sous chef at Clinton Hill’s Place des Fêtes. Chef Kawabata’s menu offers a selection of light bites and a few entrees with French and Japanese influences. The cocktail menu at Susu’s will offer “modern classics” and specialities like the Pencil Pusher, a cocktail honoring Pencil Factory. Susu’s will also have a natural wine list that focuses on independent French producers. The press release also says that music is “central to the experience at Susu’s.” The new bar will have a hi-fi sound system and vinyl DJs on weekends. Susu’s does not have an exact opening date yet, but aims to open “right after Labor Day.”

title.rendered: NYC Reddit User Becomes Silent Superman for Greenpoint’s Local Businesses
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/11/nyc-reddit-user-becomes-silent-superman-for-greenpoints-local-businesses/
date: 2026-08-11T13:25:01
content.rendered: A few weeks ago, customers began pouring into Greenpoint staple Star Deli (176 Nassau Ave.) after a series of videos posted by Instagram creator @pov.outside unexpectedly went viral, amassing more than 4 million views. Google Reviews multiplied overnight and strangers from around the world donated more than $6,000 to repair the deli’s broken freezer. Behind the sudden surge was Shuayb Qureshi, a software engineer at Meta by trade and champion of neighborhood businesses by choice. Qureshi has quietly become Greenpoint’s very own Superman, putting on his cape with the mission of aiding struggling local businesses. His account has accumulated over 15,000 followers on Instagram with his videos reaching anywhere between 5,000 to over 1 million views. On weekends and evenings after his 9-to-5, Qureshi spends his time in the community, committed to spreading the word to his followers. What inspires him the most is his family back in Toronto. “I miss that love. When I go to see these restaurants, especially once I make a video about them, I feel so much love,” he said. “I feel like they’re my parents in a lot of ways or my family.” Photos: Shuayb Qureshi Although he lives in Manhattan, Qureshi turned to Reddit to ask Greenpoint residents which businesses need help most. Hundreds responded, making the project a collaborative effort shaped by the people who know the neighborhood best. After visiting, he found himself lingering in neighborhood delis long after finishing lunch and chatting with owners instead of rushing home, something he rarely experienced elsewhere in the city. “I don’t feel the soul of trendy places in Manhattan,” he said. “But I feel like when you go to places in Greenpoint, especially places like Star Deli, there’s so much soul in that.” As his friendship with Star Deli owner Zbigniew, known as “Mr. Z,” grew, Qureshi was inspired to launch the GoFundMe and ultimately credits its success to the people. “It’s not just Americans, there’s people from Canada, people from the UK, and people from Poland donating,” he said. “It’s literally the world, and I feel so humbled because everyone participated.” It was a fitting response for a neighborhood long shaped by immigrant communities. For Qureshi, Greenpoint’s changing population is one factor behind the challenges facing longtime businesses. “With transplants, we like to go to popular places like Whole Foods and restaurants that are popping on Instagram. We’re not going to delis, so I think gentrification is an issue,” he claims. “But I do think there’s some gentrifiers, people like me who actually love family-owned places.” Greenpoint has spent years grappling with rising commercial rents , shifting demographics , and changing consumer habits, all of which have contributed to the slow disappearance of longtime neighborhood establishments . As more people discover restaurants through Instagram, TikTok, and Google Reviews instead of neighborhood word of mouth, many legacy establishments have had to adjust to a rapidly evolving business environment. Qureshi believes social media can help longtime businesses connect with newer residents. Before his video, Star Deli had very few Google Reviews. Today, it has more than 300. “If you look at people like Mr. Z, he works seven days a week, and is still trying to adapt to the new people,” Qureshi said. “But I don’t think they know how to market via social media.” Handing Mr. Z the proceeds from the fundraiser was the most rewarding moment of the project. “When I gave Mr. Z the GoFundMe money, it felt so good,” he said. “To the point where I thought I don’t know what I’ve done in life to deserve to feel this good.” Qureshi’s videos have even inspired other influencers to visit Star Deli and create videos of their own. For Qureshi, that’s the goal, not to become the face of a movement, but to create momentum that continues long after he puts down his camera. “Having this influence comes with some sort of power,” Qureshi said. “I’m getting some sort of status here, but the people who come in and donate anonymously, they’re not expecting anything, and they really truly are the real heroes here.” Treats on display at Star Deli. Photo: Shuayb Qureshi Star Deli wasn’t the only business to benefit from his efforts. Qureshi has since turned his attention to Tony’s Pizzeria (175 Nassau Ave.), another longtime family business across from Star Deli facing increased competition from trendier spots like Chrissy’s Pizza, Diamond Slice, and Paulie Gee’s Slice Shop. Tony’s owner, Salvatore Sinnona, appreciates Qureshi’s impact. “We’ve definitely seen new faces come in mentioning his videos or social media, and that kind of exposure is something money can’t always buy,” Sinnona said. “More importantly, it reminds people that supporting local businesses keeps our communities alive.” Sinnona says attracting first-time customers has become increasingly difficult. “The biggest challenge is competing with large chains and the convenience of online ordering,” he said. “People have endless choices, and many automatically go to big brands without realizing there are family-owned places making everything with pride and tradition.” Qureshi insists the internet isn’t what saved Star Deli, the people did. Every Instagram view became a customer. Every Google review became a recommendation. Every anonymous donation became an act of neighborhood solidarity. “Go talk to your local business owners,” Qureshi urges. “You never know, they might become family.”

title.rendered: New New Patch, Founded by a Greenpoint Mom, Saves Clothes One Sticker at a Time
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/11/new-new-patch-founded-by-a-greenpoint-mom-saves-clothes-one-sticker-at-a-time/
date: 2026-08-11T09:00:00
content.rendered: “It all started with a blueberry,” Prue Linehan told Greenpointers . Linehan, a Greenpoint local and mom of two, founded a company right here in the neighborhood. It’s called New New Patch and sells Sticker Patches that save kids’ clothes from things like blueberry stains. The idea for New New Patch originated when Linehan’s four-year-old son was an infant. He loved food, but destroyed his clothes. The new mom didn’t have the time or energy for stain removal or sewing, so she would throw the clothes away because it was easier and often cheaper to buy new ones. New New Patch’s Sticker Patches. Photo: Katt Jones “When I looked for a quick fix, everything I found required an iron, sewing skills or came in the form of thick embroidered patches. None of it felt designed for how modern families actually live,” Linehan explained. Her search for an easy option began. Specifically, she wanted a peel and stick fabric patch that stuck to cotton kids’ clothes and lasted in the wash. Linehan cold-called adhesive manufacturers asking for samples, turned her living room into a research and development lab, and spent countless hours testing products. After 45 different adhesives and 1,000 test cycles later, she found one that was washable and flexible enough to become New New Patch’s signature item, Sticker Patches. New New Patch’s owner Prue Linehan at a pop-up at Giggles and Wiggles. Photo: New New Patch After 15 years of building brands and leading marketing operations for companies like Colossal Media, Etsy and Cash App, New New Patch is Linehan’s full time job. She has a manufacturing partner and access to a team of product designers for future product ideas. Linehan doesn’t have a background in material science or product engineering, but simply became “obsessed” with solving a problem that many new moms face. Linehan told Greenpointers that she didn’t do it alone. “Sticker Patches may have been invented by me, but my Greenpoint village brought it to life,” she explained. New New Patch’s illustrator, brand designer, photographer, product testers, and first child model are all in Greenpoint. Even the brand’s first retailer, resale shop Parachute (151 Norman Ave.), is local. Linehan said that the store’s team supported her throughout the three years of development and were the first to stock Sticker Patches once they launched. “People often say it takes a village to raise a child. I think it takes one to build a business, too,” Linehan mused. “New New Patch is full of fingerprints from people who believed in the idea long before anyone else did.” New New Patch’s Sticker Patches. Photo: Katt Jones The Greenpoint-built New New Patch is about making something you already have feel new again. It can turn a stained piece into something your child wants to wear all over again. “So it’s not new. It’s New New,” Linehan explained. “Our mission is to help families keep what they have in wear and make that choice as easy as buying new.” New New Patch plans to expand beyond Sticker Patches through collaborations with artists and brands while exploring ways repair can become integrated into products from the start. “Imagine buying a kids’ T-shirt that comes with a matching Sticker Patch sheet, so when the first stain inevitably happens, you already have everything you need,” Linehan said.

title.rendered: Greenpoint Elected Officials Ask NYC Parks to Reconsider Controversial Transmitter Park Proposal
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/10/greenpoint-elected-officials-ask-nyc-parks-to-reconsider-controversial-transmitter-park-proposal/
date: 2026-08-10T12:44:07
content.rendered: Greenpoint’s elected officials are speaking out against plans to construct a restaurant, marina, and public restroom at Transmitter Park , ahead of a public hearing scheduled for today, August 10. City Council Member Lincoln Restler, State Senator Kristen Gonzalez, and State Assemblymember Emily Gallagher asked the NYC Parks Department to reconsider the controversial proposal, which sparked widespread backlash when Greenpointers first revealed the plans in July. “Given our community’s nearly unanimous and vociferous opposition, we respectfully request that Parks pull back this proposal and work with our offices and community to identify a better path forward,” the letter reads. “We do not believe the maritime concession is appropriate for this park and would suggest a smaller footprint for a concession in the park — or no concession at all.” WNYC Transmitter Park Letter – Aug 10 2026 Download During an info session on August 6, several community members asserted that the larger-scale project was not a worthy trade-off for a public restroom, fearing it was a Trojan Horse that could usher in larger crowds and disturb the park’s peaceful waterfront vibe. Two restaurant industry veterans, Josh Cohen and David Schmitt, were poised to sign a 25-year license agreement that allows for the development, operation, and maintenance of a restaurant and marina in the park. A preliminary draft revealed that plans could entail the park’s current building and erecting a new two-story building in its place. The arrangement is through the Parks’ concessions program, which allows food service professionals to operate restaurants and cafes on Parks’ properties. In return, that revenue stream frees up Parks to finally build a public restroom in the park, a long-overdue goal for many in the neighborhood. And if you’re wondering, “Well, why can’t Parks just decide to build a bathroom as is?” The answer is that building public restrooms in New York City is a bit more complicated (and expensive) than it may appear. A public hearing will take place today at 2:30 p.m. (255 Greenwich St., 8th Floor, Manhattan). You can also email written testimony to fcrc@mocs.nyc.gov .

title.rendered: Williamsburg Is Getting Two 100% Affordable Housing Buildings
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/07/williamsburg-is-getting-two-100-affordable-housing-buildings/
date: 2026-08-07T09:00:00
content.rendered: Luxury towers and marinas are not the only developments coming to North Brooklyn. Affordable housing is also on the way. The Slate Property Group and the RiseBoro Community Partnership recently announced that they had secured financing to build two 100% affordable housing buildings in Williamsburg, at 178 Montrose Avenue. and 73 Meserole Street. The project consists of one 14-story and one 13-story building that will deliver 312 units – 163 in one building and 149 in the other. 60% of the units will be reserved for formerly homeless people. The rest will be affordable to low-income households, the developers said. “As one of the city’s most expensive neighborhoods, Williamsburg has been out of reach for far too many working families,” said David Schwartz, co-founder and principal of Slate Property Group, during the announcement covered by the New York Real Estate Journal . “This project aims to fix that.” The development will include a mix of unit sizes, from studios to three-bedroom apartments, with rents starting at $1,339 a month for studios to $2,304 for three-bedroom apartments. Designed by Aufgang Architects, the all-electric buildings will offer social services on site, as well as amenities including outdoor and indoor recreation areas, fitness facilities and laundry. RiseBoro is to provide full-time security and maintenance staff. The project adds to a growing supply of affordable housing announced for Greenpoint and Williamsburg over the last month. Recently announced developments include projects on Oak Street and the Broadway Triangle as well as the affordable housing commitment for Monitor Point project . Altogether, these developments are expected to bring more than 2,000 affordable units to the area.

content.rendered: Our “Greenpoint at Work” interview series takes a look at the lives of people living and/or working in the Greenpoint-Williamsburg area. We recently spoke to Radamés Millán, who has been running San Germán Records at 89 Moore Street in Williamsburg since the 1970s, offering Puerto Rican souvenirs, clothes, food and music. Read our most recent “Greenpoint at Work” profile here . This interview has been edited and condensed for clarity. Tell us about yourself and how you founded San Germán Records. I’m Puerto Rican, born and raised in the town of San Germán, a small but culturally rich community in the southwest of the island. From a young age, I enjoyed being involved in my community. I often organized benefit events to support young people in my hometown. Those experiences taught me the value of bringing people together. About 70 years ago, I moved to New York and settled on Long Island. I got my first job working in a restaurant. At the time, my English was very limited, and to be honest, I wasn’t very enthusiastic about learning the language. Still, I worked my way up. I stayed at that restaurant for more than 10 years. Eventually I felt ready to become a manager, but the opportunity never came. That’s when I decided to start my own business. I began selling LP records door to door on Long Island. Later, a friend suggested I move my business to Brooklyn. I took his advice, and that’s how I ended up here, and it’s been fantastic. San Germán Records was originally a record store. Yes, we’ve done so many great things over the years that I would never have dreamed of. I founded a record label, where we recorded a great deal of traditional Puerto Rican music. Promoting and preserving that music has always been very important to me. The albums we produced, along with records from many other labels, were what we sold in the store. At that time, Williamsburg had a large Puerto Rican population, so Puerto Rican music was by far the most popular. Around 80% of the people living in this area were Puerto Rican. In the 1970s and 1980s, reggaeton didn’t exist yet, or if it did, it was still very underground. Salsa became more popular later. The music people wanted to listen to was traditional Puerto Rican music, mainly because many of the families living here had come from rural parts of the island, where those musical traditions were deeply rooted. Traditional Puerto Rican music such as jíbara, bomba or plena features instruments like the cuatro, guitars, maracas and bongos. Boleros were also very popular, especially recordings by trios like Los Panchos and Los Condes. That was the soundtrack of the community during those years. Back then, the Puerto Rican community was vibrant and highly visible . Unfortunately, gentrification changed the neighborhood. Many families who had bought houses here at affordable prices eventually received attractive offers and decided to sell and left. Most of them have moved to Florida. A few of us have stayed. I decided not to throw in the towel, and I’m still here. Photo: Greenpointers In what ways have you continued to organize community events over the decades? One of the most important events we organize is the annual Three Kings Parade. We created it to celebrate and share the biblical tradition of the Three Kings, which is observed every January 6 and is deeply rooted in Puerto Rican culture. I experienced that tradition firsthand as a child, and I’ve always been committed to keeping it alive here in Brooklyn. In 2027, we’ll celebrate the parade’s 30th anniversary. The event will take place on January 10 along Graham Avenue, and we’re planning something very special to mark this milestone. The parade is a day filled with live music, dancers, colorful floats and vibrant costumes. Over the years, we’ve welcomed many headline performers. Some of the biggest names in salsa have participated, along with artists such as Daddy Yankee, La India, Tito Rojas and Jerry Rivera, just to name a few. Given that San Germán Records has evolved over the years, what sets it apart today? Today my store offers all kinds of products that celebrate Puerto Rican culture and traditions. Visitors can find traditional candies and foods, including Puerto Rican cake and arroz con dulce along with a wide variety of souvenirs. Of course, we have the coquí in every form imaginable. It would be unthinkable not to. The coquí is a tiny frog native to Puerto Rico, famous for the distinctive sound it makes at night, and it has become one of the island’s most recognizable symbols. We also carry countless items featuring the Puerto Rican flag. Another popular item is the pava, the traditional straw hat worn by Puerto Rican farmers to protect themselves from the intense Caribbean sun, especially during the years when sugar cane was one of the island’s main crops. More recently, the pava has gained new visibility thanks to Bad Bunny. Sports memorabilia is also an important part of what we offer. Puerto Rico has produced legendary athletes, particularly in boxing and baseball. Figures like Félix “Tito” Trinidad and Roberto Clemente have become icons far beyond the island. Visitors can find jerseys and memorabilia honoring these and many other sports legends. For Puerto Ricans, these items are more than souvenirs. They reconnect us with our memories, our heritage and our identity. Boricuas are very proud of where we come from, so anything that represents the island is sacred for us. Photo: Greenpointers What are your plans for continuing to promote community events? Before talking about plans, I’d like to mention something important. As Williamsburg has undergone gentrification, some of the newer residents have tried to erase parts of the neighborhood’s Puerto Rican history. In 2023, the city Department of Transportation removed the street signs identifying Graham Avenue as “Avenue of Puerto Rico,” even though that honorary designation had been approved decades earlier by the New York City Council. For our community, those signs recognize the generations of Puerto Ricans who lived here and whose contributions helped shape Williamsburg’s identity. I was proud to be part of the effort to have those signs reinstated. Many Puerto Ricans believe their removal reflected real estate interests. We think that perhaps some developers or property owners would rather not tell prospective tenants they’re moving to a neighborhood whose main street is known as “Avenue of Puerto Rico” because that would create fear among certain clients. One of the ways we responded was by bringing the National Puerto Rican Day Parade back to Williamsburg in 2023. Although it was held for only one year, it was incredibly meaningful for the community. My hope is to make that parade a permanent tradition once again, specifically on Graham Avenue. There is already a Puerto Rican Day Parade on Knickerbocker Avenue in Bushwick, but in my opinion, Graham Avenue is the natural home for this celebration . It’s the only street officially recognized as the Avenue of Puerto Rico, and it’s where the original parade was held. Unfortunately, we couldn’t continue after 2023 because of budget constraints. But if we can secure the necessary support and sponsorship, I’d love to bring it back. If individuals, organizations or businesses are interested in helping make that happen, my door is always open. What’s coming up in the next few months for San Germán Records? In September, we host a block party right outside the store. It’s a celebration of Puerto Rican culture with live music and entertainment throughout the day. We set up a stage on the street, and the festivities run from 12 to 6 pm. This year, the block party will take place on September 12, and everyone’s welcome.
