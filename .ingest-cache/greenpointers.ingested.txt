content.rendered: On August 10, Colombia was hit by a 7.4 magnitude earthquake , leaving nearly 300 people dead, hundreds missing and nearly 4,000 injured. With five capital cities placed on red alert amid widespread destruction, the South American country is in urgent need of international aid. In response, the Debevoise Healthcare Center (5 Debevoise St.) in Williamsburg has turned part of its space into a temporary donation center. They are accepting new and unopened items including: Emergency supplies (the most urgently needed) : flashlights, new batteries, portable power banks, N95 masks, work gloves and emergency blankets. First aid supplies: first aid kits, bandages, medical tape, antiseptic wipes, disposable gloves and cleaning supplies like medical tape, heavy-duty garbage bags, cleaning gloves, disinfectant wipes, paper towels, small cleaning supplies. Baby supplies : diapers, baby wipes, sealed formula and diaper-rash cream. Hygiene products: soap, toothbrushes, shampoo, feminine hygiene products and body wipes. “We are not accepting food donations, so we would appreciate it if people could donate the items on our list,” Dr. Gina Cespedes, who has an office at the health center, told Greenpointers . She said donors do not need to call ahead before dropping off items. Donations can be brought to the reception desk Monday through Friday, from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. The medical center will accept donations through August 21. The collection is part of a broader relief effort coordinated by the Colombian American Police Association (CAPA), Colombianos en New York , and the Seventh-day Adventist Church. A full list of the items they are accepting, as well as other drop-off locations across the city can be found on Fuerza Colombia . Those who would like to volunteer can also register through the site, as help is needed to pack and organize donations.

content.rendered: This September, a new neighborhood bar called Susu’s is opening at 229 Kent Avenue in Williamsburg, in the former home of Grand Ferry , a tavern that closed in 2018. Susu’s will be an easy-going spot that serves cocktails, natural wine, and bar food. Susu’s calls itself “a bar made by bartenders.” The owners are Steven Bishop and his brother Michael Bishop-Myers, who was the general manager of Pencil Factory (142 Franklin St.), the beloved local bar that closed last summer. Several Pencil Factory staff members followed Bishop-Myers and are part of the opening team at Susu’s. The owners said that the idea behind Susu’s is that customers shouldn’t have to fight for a reservation and that getting a good drink shouldn’t be difficult. “I’ve been bartending in New York for basically a decade. I just wanted to build the bar my friends and I actually want to drink in,” said owner Michael Bishop-Myers in a press release. “No host, no reservations, you walk in and I make you a drink.” The press release calls Susu’s “easygoing like a corner dive,” but “with food and drinks that belong somewhere much fancier.” The kitchen is helmed by Chef Garrett Kawabata, a former sous chef at Clinton Hill’s Place des Fêtes. Chef Kawabata’s menu offers a selection of light bites and a few entrees with French and Japanese influences. The cocktail menu at Susu’s will offer “modern classics” and specialities like the Pencil Pusher, a cocktail honoring Pencil Factory. Susu’s will also have a natural wine list that focuses on independent French producers. The press release also says that music is “central to the experience at Susu’s.” The new bar will have a hi-fi sound system and vinyl DJs on weekends. Susu’s does not have an exact opening date yet, but aims to open “right after Labor Day.”

title.rendered: NYC Reddit User Becomes Silent Superman for Greenpoint’s Local Businesses
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/11/nyc-reddit-user-becomes-silent-superman-for-greenpoints-local-businesses/
date: 2026-08-11T13:25:01
content.rendered: A few weeks ago, customers began pouring into Greenpoint staple Star Deli (176 Nassau Ave.) after a series of videos posted by Instagram creator @pov.outside unexpectedly went viral, amassing more than 4 million views. Google Reviews multiplied overnight and strangers from around the world donated more than $6,000 to repair the deli’s broken freezer. Behind the sudden surge was Shuayb Qureshi, a software engineer at Meta by trade and champion of neighborhood businesses by choice. Qureshi has quietly become Greenpoint’s very own Superman, putting on his cape with the mission of aiding struggling local businesses. His account has accumulated over 15,000 followers on Instagram with his videos reaching anywhere between 5,000 to over 1 million views. On weekends and evenings after his 9-to-5, Qureshi spends his time in the community, committed to spreading the word to his followers. What inspires him the most is his family back in Toronto. “I miss that love. When I go to see these restaurants, especially once I make a video about them, I feel so much love,” he said. “I feel like they’re my parents in a lot of ways or my family.” Photos: Shuayb Qureshi Although he lives in Manhattan, Qureshi turned to Reddit to ask Greenpoint residents which businesses need help most. Hundreds responded, making the project a collaborative effort shaped by the people who know the neighborhood best. After visiting, he found himself lingering in neighborhood delis long after finishing lunch and chatting with owners instead of rushing home, something he rarely experienced elsewhere in the city. “I don’t feel the soul of trendy places in Manhattan,” he said. “But I feel like when you go to places in Greenpoint, especially places like Star Deli, there’s so much soul in that.” As his friendship with Star Deli owner Zbigniew, known as “Mr. Z,” grew, Qureshi was inspired to launch the GoFundMe and ultimately credits its success to the people. “It’s not just Americans, there’s people from Canada, people from the UK, and people from Poland donating,” he said. “It’s literally the world, and I feel so humbled because everyone participated.” It was a fitting response for a neighborhood long shaped by immigrant communities. For Qureshi, Greenpoint’s changing population is one factor behind the challenges facing longtime businesses. “With transplants, we like to go to popular places like Whole Foods and restaurants that are popping on Instagram. We’re not going to delis, so I think gentrification is an issue,” he claims. “But I do think there’s some gentrifiers, people like me who actually love family-owned places.” Greenpoint has spent years grappling with rising commercial rents , shifting demographics , and changing consumer habits, all of which have contributed to the slow disappearance of longtime neighborhood establishments . As more people discover restaurants through Instagram, TikTok, and Google Reviews instead of neighborhood word of mouth, many legacy establishments have had to adjust to a rapidly evolving business environment. Qureshi believes social media can help longtime businesses connect with newer residents. Before his video, Star Deli had very few Google Reviews. Today, it has more than 300. “If you look at people like Mr. Z, he works seven days a week, and is still trying to adapt to the new people,” Qureshi said. “But I don’t think they know how to market via social media.” Handing Mr. Z the proceeds from the fundraiser was the most rewarding moment of the project. “When I gave Mr. Z the GoFundMe money, it felt so good,” he said. “To the point where I thought I don’t know what I’ve done in life to deserve to feel this good.” Qureshi’s videos have even inspired other influencers to visit Star Deli and create videos of their own. For Qureshi, that’s the goal, not to become the face of a movement, but to create momentum that continues long after he puts down his camera. “Having this influence comes with some sort of power,” Qureshi said. “I’m getting some sort of status here, but the people who come in and donate anonymously, they’re not expecting anything, and they really truly are the real heroes here.” Treats on display at Star Deli. Photo: Shuayb Qureshi Star Deli wasn’t the only business to benefit from his efforts. Qureshi has since turned his attention to Tony’s Pizzeria (175 Nassau Ave.), another longtime family business across from Star Deli facing increased competition from trendier spots like Chrissy’s Pizza, Diamond Slice, and Paulie Gee’s Slice Shop. Tony’s owner, Salvatore Sinnona, appreciates Qureshi’s impact. “We’ve definitely seen new faces come in mentioning his videos or social media, and that kind of exposure is something money can’t always buy,” Sinnona said. “More importantly, it reminds people that supporting local businesses keeps our communities alive.” Sinnona says attracting first-time customers has become increasingly difficult. “The biggest challenge is competing with large chains and the convenience of online ordering,” he said. “People have endless choices, and many automatically go to big brands without realizing there are family-owned places making everything with pride and tradition.” Qureshi insists the internet isn’t what saved Star Deli, the people did. Every Instagram view became a customer. Every Google review became a recommendation. Every anonymous donation became an act of neighborhood solidarity. “Go talk to your local business owners,” Qureshi urges. “You never know, they might become family.”

title.rendered: New New Patch, Founded by a Greenpoint Mom, Saves Clothes One Sticker at a Time
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/11/new-new-patch-founded-by-a-greenpoint-mom-saves-clothes-one-sticker-at-a-time/

content.rendered: “It all started with a blueberry,” Prue Linehan told Greenpointers . Linehan, a Greenpoint local and mom of two, founded a company right here in the neighborhood. It’s called New New Patch and sells Sticker Patches that save kids’ clothes from things like blueberry stains. The idea for New New Patch originated when Linehan’s four-year-old son was an infant. He loved food, but destroyed his clothes. The new mom didn’t have the time or energy for stain removal or sewing, so she would throw the clothes away because it was easier and often cheaper to buy new ones. New New Patch’s Sticker Patches. Photo: Katt Jones “When I looked for a quick fix, everything I found required an iron, sewing skills or came in the form of thick embroidered patches. None of it felt designed for how modern families actually live,” Linehan explained. Her search for an easy option began. Specifically, she wanted a peel and stick fabric patch that stuck to cotton kids’ clothes and lasted in the wash. Linehan cold-called adhesive manufacturers asking for samples, turned her living room into a research and development lab, and spent countless hours testing products. After 45 different adhesives and 1,000 test cycles later, she found one that was washable and flexible enough to become New New Patch’s signature item, Sticker Patches. New New Patch’s owner Prue Linehan at a pop-up at Giggles and Wiggles. Photo: New New Patch After 15 years of building brands and leading marketing operations for companies like Colossal Media, Etsy and Cash App, New New Patch is Linehan’s full time job. She has a manufacturing partner and access to a team of product designers for future product ideas. Linehan doesn’t have a background in material science or product engineering, but simply became “obsessed” with solving a problem that many new moms face. Linehan told Greenpointers that she didn’t do it alone. “Sticker Patches may have been invented by me, but my Greenpoint village brought it to life,” she explained. New New Patch’s illustrator, brand designer, photographer, product testers, and first child model are all in Greenpoint. The Slate Property Group and the RiseBoro Community Partnership recently announced that they had secured financing to build two 100% affordable housing buildings in Williamsburg, at 178 Montrose Avenue. and 73 Meserole Street. The project consists of one 14-story and one 13-story building that will deliver 312 units – 163 in one building and 149 in the other. 60% of the units will be reserved for formerly homeless people. The rest will be affordable to low-income households, the developers said. “As one of the city’s most expensive neighborhoods, Williamsburg has been out of reach for far too many working families,” said David Schwartz, co-founder and principal of Slate Property Group, during the announcement covered by the New York Real Estate Journal . “This project aims to fix that.” The development will include a mix of unit sizes, from studios to three-bedroom apartments, with rents starting at $1,339 a month for studios to $2,304 for three-bedroom apartments. Designed by Aufgang Architects, the all-electric buildings will offer social services on site, as well as amenities including outdoor and indoor recreation areas, fitness facilities and laundry. RiseBoro is to provide full-time security and maintenance staff. The project adds to a growing supply of affordable housing announced for Greenpoint and Williamsburg over the last month. Recently announced developments include projects on Oak Street and the Broadway Triangle as well as the affordable housing commitment for Monitor Point project . Altogether, these developments are expected to bring more than 2,000 affordable units to the area.

title.rendered: Archestratus, Bookstore and Community Hub, Will Close This Spring
date: 2026-02-23T11:18:02
content.rendered: Archestratus (164 Huron St.), the beloved book store feeding Greenpoint for 10 years, will officially close this spring. Owner Paige Lipari shared the decision in an Instagram post last Friday, stating that the store’s last day will be on April 26. Everything in the store will be on sale until they close. Lipari cited several reasons for the closure. “We hopscotched from the best Christmas in our history (2024) to our worst spring last year. Followed by our worst summer, our worst Fall, and a Christmas that was 30 to 40 percent down,” she wrote. “We also simply can’t afford our own food products due to tariffs and rising costs,” echoing a sentiment shared by so many small business owners we’ve spoken to recently. The Wild Honey Pie’s Dinners at Archestratus in 2017. Photo: The Wild Honey Pie Lipari started Archestratus in 2015, as a cookbook store and cafe. The store quickly became a hub for not only the neighborhood, but for the city’s food scene at large. Countless chefs, cooks, and authors passed through those doors. Archestratus frequently hosted events such as cookbook clubs, cookie making workshops, and food writing classes (not to mention, so many community bake sales!) The store expanded into an additional storefront in 2021, with one side serving as the cafe and grocery store, and the other serving as the bookstore. They eventually scaled back again, eliminating the cafe ( Rita’s Pizza & Provisions took over that space, though their tenure was short-lived, soon it will be Herbie’s Burgers ). Archestratus represented the best of Greenpoint—scrappy, creative, and community-oriented. We’ve already been missing those sandwiches, but now we’ll miss everything else, too.
