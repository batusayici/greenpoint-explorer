title.rendered: How Murawski Pharmacy Is Keeping Late Owner’s Legacy Alive

content.rendered: Murawski Pharmacy has been a cornerstone of the Greenpoint community for nearly five decades. Owner Timothy Murawski sadly passed away on July 10 , and his family is working to honor his legacy. He and his brother Kenneth opened the store 48 years ago, first in a space across the street, but soon finding a home at 94-98 Nassau Avenue. The brothers were born and raised Brooklynites, though they had Polish roots. They hoped to connect the area’s Polish population with products from home. “Throughout the history of the pharmacy, the main business model has been just catering to people and catering to what they left behind in Poland and replicating their community in their very niche corner of Brooklyn, in the pharmacy,” Timothy’s son Matthew tells Greenpointers , noting the wide array of products they stock. Customers can find magazines, puzzles, greeting cards, and even a Western Union. Most of the staff speaks Polish, not a requirement, but a show of the store’s dedication to keeping the area’s cultural heritage alive. Several employees have even worked there for decades. Photo courtesy of Matthew Murawski. Timothy’s dry wit and humor, as well as his rapport with customers, left a mark on that corner of Nassau Avenue. “I know so many customers who came to send packages, came to send money, and they would sit there and chat about history or anecdotal stories from their life, and he would always have something to say about it,” said Matthew, who has worked there off and on. “They would feel respected; they would feel appreciated and understood.” Running a small business always comes with unique challenges, but several factors make running an independent pharmacy more difficult than ever in 2026. Tariffs increase the cost of importing products from overseas, a hallmark of Murawski’s business. And bigger chains moving into Greenpoint draw business away from the mom-and-pop operations. A PBS report from 2025 found that “[f]rom 2010 to 2021, 30% of drugstores in the U.S. closed,” with the problem increasing in 2024. “In 2024 alone, about 2,300 pharmacies closed , half of them being independent or mid-sized businesses.” What’s more, the monopolization of pharmacy benefit managers (firms that act as middlemen between manufacturers, insurance companies, and pharmacies) has spelled catastrophe for these smaller businesses. These managers often slash reimbursement rates to lower drug costs, and that shrinking rate can hurt an independent pharmacy’s bottom line. For a small, family-owned business like Murawski, customer support means everything. So stop in, whether to pick up a prescription or for a friendly chat.

title.rendered: The Final 1,000 Days of Barcade Williamsburg
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/24/the-final-1000-days-of-barcade-williamsburg/

content.rendered: Last month, Barcade posted a sort of “in memoriam” about the early days of their inaugural location on Union Avenue in Williamsburg—where they’ve remained since 2004. In that same post, the arcade bar also gave notice that their lease is ending in under 1,000 days. Obviously, a lot has changed since then, which they acknowledge themselves: “Barcade in Brooklyn opened on October 14, 2004, in a gritty part of Williamsburg. Some of our neighbors were a beer distributor and an empty warehouse across the street that housed a bootleg skate park inside. The neighborhood has changed a lot in 22 years! Now, as nearly all the brick and wood buildings around us have been demolished and rebuilt in steel and glass, and the nightlife has mostly faded away, we know the end is coming soon.” The original Barcade building (source: @barcade on Instagram) The building itself was a metal shop before Barcade moved in, representing an other and even older era of the neighborhood. The post was meant to be less of a firm closing notice and more of an ode to a bygone era (from breaking up bar fights at 4 a.m. to craft beer events with Bell’s, Rogue, and beyond), while also hoping to help people avoid the common ‘don’t-know-what-you’ve-got-’till-it’s-gone’ pitfalls and give them ample time to visit for a beer, chat, or round of Frogger. “We got to a thousand days left in our lease and we thought it was good timing to make a tongue-in-cheek post reminding people that our lease is ending,” Barcade CEO and Co-Founder Paul Kermizian explained. He also cites their Newark and Jersey City locations as inspiration for the post, since he recalled regulars and customers being upset about the shorter (or lack of) notice when they closed. “It’s such a luxury to have a business that people care about so much that they get mad at you for not giving enough notice for closing,” he laughed. “So, so we just saw the opportunity to be like, ‘Hey, it’d be great to remind people that we’re not gonna be here forever.'” The post garnered many comments across social media platforms with sentiments ranging from “there goes the neighborhood” to “where else will I play Marvel vs Capcom 2?” and plenty of discourse around gentrification, which the team admittedly wasn’t expecting when they set it live. Above all, though, the general sentiment was sadness over the impending closure (despite the fact that the post technically never explicitly said they were closing, which Kermizian pointed out with a laugh). “While we didn’t say we’re definitely closing, I think it’s a pretty safe bet that we won’t be staying past the end of the lease,” he said. “Our landlords have been great and very fair. We don’t really know what they’re doing with the building, but we can imagine that it probably doesn’t make sense for us to stay in that spot. That’s what I was getting at in the post. Nightlife in the area’s not what it used to be.” One major factor is the overall decline in alcohol consumption—according to an annual Gallup poll , the number of Americans over 18 who say they drink (54%) has been at a record low since last year (its lowest level since before World War II). 17% also have substituted non-alcoholic drinks for alcohol. The number of people who think 1 to 2 drinks per day is bad for your health has nearly doubled since 2001. Also, owners have noticed less spillover from other local bars as a result But, while Kermizian and the team are all beer enthusiasts, it’s not all that Barcade has to offer. There are, of course, the games, which are constantly rotating as gaming trends change and rare models become available. There is also a growing selection of zero-proof drinks. And there are still plenty of days left to enjoy it all, but the time will pass quickly! As for the future, any business owner (and most humans) will note that it’s nearly impossible to predict what three years from now will look like, though the team would love to stay somewhere in Brooklyn if it makes sense. But for the nearer future, customers can expect some more special events, old-fashioned rare keg openings, and an eventual closing bash when the time comes. “When we’re done, it’ll be a 25-year run in the neighborhood,” Kermizian said. “Naturally, things are gonna change in any neighborhood in that amount of time. It’s a real privilege to have a business that people have that kind of enthusiasm for. So it’ll be bittersweet to close, but it’s a great run and no regrets about anything.”

title.rendered: Greenpoint’s Transmitter Park to Get New Restaurant and Marina
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/07/28/greenpoints-transmitter-park-to-get-new-restaurant-and-marina/
date: 2026-07-28T11:39:15
content.rendered: Greenpoint’s WNYC Transmitter Park will be the home of a new restaurant and a marina with free programming, Greenpointers can exclusively share. Two local industry veterans, Josh Cohen and David Schmitt, are working with the NYC Parks and Recreation Department and preparing to sign a 25-year license agreement that allows for the development, operation, and maintenance of the restaurant and marina.  No names have been finalized yet for this project, but Cohen said it “will be an homage to Transmitter.”



 A mock-up of the “license premises” in the license agreement for the Transmitter Park project. Photo: City of New York



 Schmitt and Cohen are seasoned professionals with restaurant industry experience. The pair told Greenpointers that they met about six months ago and hit it off instantly. Cohen is a longtime North Brooklyn resident who is very involved in the local community and with organizations like North Brooklyn Angels . Alongside other partners, Cohen has ownership in Chez Ma Tante , Lilia, Saint Vitus , and Dead Letter No. 9 . He also ran neighborhood favorites Jimmy’s Diner , Anella , and Extra Fancy, which are now closed. Schmitt owns Dakota’s on the Cove in the Bronx and DJ’s at Bayside in Queens, both waterfront restaurants overlooking marinas, very similar to the new Transmitter Park project. 



 Schmitt also owns marinas and has worked with NYC Parks’ properties before. He is excited to work with the department again to “add to the existing infrastructure in Transmitter.”



 The possible site of Transmitter Park’s new restaurant can be seen behind a member of Friends of Transmitter Park. Photo: Friends of Transmitter Park



 The timeline of the Transmitter Park project, as detailed on the current draft of the license agreement , begins with overall design.



 Alexander Han, Chief of Concessions for NYC Parks, told Greenpointers that his department is currently working with David Schmitt and his team to develop a design that is “complementary to park.”  Han said that the public will have the opportunity to review designs and provide input during a public hearing next month. The license agreement states that the second phase of the Transmitter Park project includes the demolition of the abandoned building that’s located inside the park, followed by the third phase which includes building a new structure for the restaurant with a public restroom, something that local volunteer organization Friends of Transmitter Park has been fighting for . According to the current draft of the license agreement, the restaurant will serve lunch and dinner daily from May 1 to October 31. From November though April, dinner will be offered during week with lunch and dinner on weekends. An “enclosed lounge” on the second floor will be open from May through October in the evenings. Han explained that all of these details could change. Formal designs have not be submitted and approved. For example, the entire abandoned building might not be leveled; engineers and architects are evaluating the current structure.  Han confirmed that the restaurant will include maintained public restrooms, storage space for gardening materials and other things, and programs for the community similar to the offerings at McCarren Parkhouse .



 The possible future home of the new restaurant inside Transmitter Park is on the right.



 The fourth and final phase of the Transmitter Park project is the construction of the marina “with up to 40 slips including pilings, floating docks, utilities, and safety/security systems.” In addition to boats, the marina will have rentals of “non-motorized” things like kayaks and paddle boards. Unlike Bushwick Inlet Park’s Motiva parcel , Transmitter Park will not have a beach. Instead, water access and activities will happen via the pier. As the agreement currently stands, the marina would be open seasonally from May 1 to October 31. Programming listed on the license includes things like like boat tours and educational programming for both kids and adults. At Schmitt’s marina in Queens, he offers things the Bayside Snapper Derby , a free fishing event for kids, and hopes to have programs like this in Transmitter Park. “It’s all free programming, and it fills your heart,” Schmitt said. Han told Greenpointers that the pier currently located at Transmitter will remain open to the public. “No one is losing access. No part of these plans will take away from public,” Han said. “Our goal is to enhance waterfront access.” Schmitt told Greenpointers that he is excited to expand Greenpoint’s access to the water. “Even though Greenpoint is located on the water, there is currently limited accessibility,” Schmitt highlighted. 



 Fishing on Transmitter Park’s current pier. Photo: Friends of Transmitter Park



 Elissa Iberti, Friends of Transmitter Park Chair, said that the group is happy that this stage of the project has been reached and a food vendor has been found. However, the group’s concern is the scale of the project. She does not want the park to lose its identity and believes that the current proposal does not feel in balance with Transmitter Park’s small acreage.  “We are excited to have a partner and don’t want to shut this down, just address concerns. This is all about the Greenpoint community,” Iberti stated.  Friends of Transmitter Park also has “environmental concerns.”  Iberti wonders if Transmitter Park is the right place for a 40-slip marina, but believes “some form of this project could work.” Han said that the design has not been approved, so the “scale and size is not final.” Han called the proposed restaurant “appropriate for the park,” but thinks it’s too early to comment on the marina phase, which will go though another round of approvals with the opportunity for the public to review. Iberti is also concerned that the money the City receives from the license will go to a general fund, and not directly to Parks or Transmitter Park specifically. Han confirmed that this is the case. “We believe it is an investment in the Greenpoint neighborhood,” Han said. “We are happy to listen to Friends of Transmitter and work with them.”



 Transmitter Park in Greenpoint. Photo: Friends of Transmitter Park



 “We are honored to be a part of this and to add to what Friends of Transmitter Park has already built,” Cohen and Schmitt told Greenpointers . “This is a fantastic opportunity to give back to community.” “We plan on working with community input throughout the process. It has great potential and will help reinvest into Transmitter,” Cohen added, noting that the park is his “backyard” also. The Transmitter Park project is still in its “infant” stage. The projected date of completion is January 2029, but Cohen and Schmitt hope to have things open earlier than that. Stay tuned for more updates over the next two to three years.

title.rendered: NYC Reddit User Becomes Silent Superman for Greenpoint’s Local Businesses
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/11/nyc-reddit-user-becomes-silent-superman-for-greenpoints-local-businesses/
date: 2026-08-11T13:25:01
content.rendered: A few weeks ago, customers began pouring into Greenpoint staple Star Deli (176 Nassau Ave.) after a series of videos posted by Instagram creator @pov.outside unexpectedly went viral, amassing more than 4 million views. Google Reviews multiplied overnight and strangers from around the world donated more than $6,000 to repair the deli’s broken freezer. Behind the sudden surge was Shuayb Qureshi, a software engineer at Meta by trade and champion of neighborhood businesses by choice. Qureshi has quietly become Greenpoint’s very own Superman, putting on his cape with the mission of aiding struggling local businesses. His account has accumulated over 15,000 followers on Instagram with his videos reaching anywhere between 5,000 to over 1 million views. On weekends and evenings after his 9-to-5, Qureshi spends his time in the community, committed to spreading the word to his followers. What inspires him the most is his family back in Toronto. “I miss that love. When I go to see these restaurants, especially once I make a video about them, I feel so much love,” he said. “I feel like they’re my parents in a lot of ways or my family.”









 Photos: Shuayb Qureshi



 Although he lives in Manhattan, Qureshi turned to Reddit to ask Greenpoint residents which businesses need help most. Hundreds responded, making the project a collaborative effort shaped by the people who know the neighborhood best.  After visiting, he found himself lingering in neighborhood delis long after finishing lunch and chatting with owners instead of rushing home, something he rarely experienced elsewhere in the city.  “I don’t feel the soul of trendy places in Manhattan,” he said. “But I feel like when you go to places in Greenpoint, especially places like Star Deli, there’s so much soul in that.” As his friendship with Star Deli owner Zbigniew, known as “Mr. Z,” grew, Qureshi was inspired to launch the GoFundMe and ultimately credits its success to the people.  “It’s not just Americans, there’s people from Canada, people from the UK, and people from Poland donating,” he said. “It’s literally the world, and I feel so humbled because everyone participated.” It was a fitting response for a neighborhood long shaped by immigrant communities. For Qureshi, Greenpoint’s changing population is one factor behind the challenges facing longtime businesses. “With transplants, we like to go to popular places like Whole Foods and restaurants that are popping on Instagram. We’re not going to delis, so I think gentrification is an issue,” he claims. “But I do think there’s some gentrifiers, people like me who actually love family-owned places.”



 Greenpoint has spent years grappling with rising commercial rents , shifting demographics , and changing consumer habits, all of which have contributed to the slow disappearance of longtime neighborhood establishments . As more people discover restaurants through Instagram, TikTok, and Google Reviews instead of neighborhood word of mouth, many legacy establishments have had to adjust to a rapidly evolving business environment.  Qureshi believes social media can help longtime businesses connect with newer residents. Before his video, Star Deli had very few Google Reviews. Today, it has more than 300. “If you look at people like Mr. Z, he works seven days a week, and is still trying to adapt to the new people,” Qureshi said. “But I don’t think they know how to market via social media.” Handing Mr. Z the proceeds from the fundraiser was the most rewarding moment of the project. “When I gave Mr. Z the GoFundMe money, it felt so good,” he said. “To the point where I thought I don’t know what I’ve done in life to deserve to feel this good.” Qureshi’s videos have even inspired other influencers to visit Star Deli and create videos of their own. For Qureshi, that’s the goal, not to become the face of a movement, but to create momentum that continues long after he puts down his camera.  “Having this influence comes with some sort of power,” Qureshi said. “I’m getting some sort of status here, but the people who come in and donate anonymously, they’re not expecting anything, and they really truly are the real heroes here.”  



 Treats on display at Star Deli. Photo: Shuayb Qureshi



 Star Deli wasn’t the only business to benefit from his efforts. Qureshi has since turned his attention to Tony’s Pizzeria (175 Nassau Ave.), another longtime family business across from Star Deli facing increased competition from trendier spots like Chrissy’s Pizza, Diamond Slice, and Paulie Gee’s Slice Shop. Tony’s owner, Salvatore Sinnona, appreciates Qureshi’s impact.  “We’ve definitely seen new faces come in mentioning his videos or social media, and that kind of exposure is something money can’t always buy,” Sinnona said. “More importantly, it reminds people that supporting local businesses keeps our communities alive.” Sinnona says attracting first-time customers has become increasingly difficult. “The biggest challenge is competing with large chains and the convenience of online ordering,” he said. “People have endless choices, and many automatically go to big brands without realizing there are family-owned places making everything with pride and tradition.”  Qureshi insists the internet isn’t what saved Star Deli, the people did. Every Instagram view became a customer. Every Google review became a recommendation. Every anonymous donation became an act of neighborhood solidarity. “Go talk to your local business owners,” Qureshi urges. “You never know, they might become family.”

 During an info session on August 6, several community members asserted that the larger-scale project was not a worthy trade-off for a public restroom, fearing it was a Trojan Horse that could usher in larger crowds and disturb the park’s peaceful waterfront vibe.



 Two restaurant industry veterans, Josh Cohen and David Schmitt, were poised to sign a 25-year license agreement that allows for the development, operation, and maintenance of a restaurant and marina in the park. A preliminary draft revealed that plans could entail the park’s current building and erecting a new two-story building in its place.



 The arrangement is through the Parks’ concessions program, which allows food service professionals to operate restaurants and cafes on Parks’ properties. In return, that revenue stream frees up Parks to finally build a public restroom in the park, a long-overdue goal for many in the neighborhood.



 And if you’re wondering, “Well, why can’t Parks just decide to build a bathroom as is?” The answer is that building public restrooms in New York City is a bit more complicated (and expensive) than it may appear.
