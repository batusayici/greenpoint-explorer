content.rendered: After 18 years, Ashbox Cafe (1154 Manhattan Ave.) has closed its doors. Thank you for making my dream a reality for nearly two decades. Thank you for 18 years of unwavering support, friendship and love. You have blessed my life more than words can say.” Ashbox Cafe’s crispy karaage chicken. Photo: Ashbox Cafe Ashbox Cafe will host an inventory farewell sale to get rid of the eatery’s remaining stock of plates, cups, and bowls, packaged food, and kitchen tools. The sale will take place Tuesday, August 25 through Friday, August 28, 11:30 a.m. – 2:30 p.m. If you are not in need of kitchen supplies, you can stop by the sale anyway to bid farewell to a beloved chef. Kubo said she would be grateful for proper goodbyes.

title.rendered: Greenpoint Small Business Owners Call for MTA to Move December G Train Shutdown
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/20/greenpoint-small-business-owners-call-for-mta-to-move-december-g-train-shutdown/
date: 2026-08-20T12:30:00
content.rendered: Greenpoint’s small business owners rallied this morning to ask the MTA to reconsider a planned G train shutdown in December. The MTA plans to suspend G train service for at least three weekends in December, a move business owners fear will compound the stress of multiple prolonged shutdowns over the past couple of years. State Senator Kristen Gonzalez and Assemblymember Emily Gallagher both spoke during the press conference, held in front of Clay Space (275 Calyer St.). “We’re not saying that we don’t want to see the G train improve,” Gonzalez told the crowd. “What we are saying is that the schedule under which these improvements have been happening has been detrimental to the small businesses in our neighborhoods that are already struggling with the rising cost of rent, the rising cost of utilities, and certainly are the backbone of North Brooklyn.” Gonzalez called on the MTA to move the scheduled shutdown to January instead of during the prime holiday shopping season. “We’re often treated as an afterthought here, and we really need to be prioritized so that our small businesses can thrive,” said Gallagher. Perri Salka speaks during the press conference. Photo: Greenpointers The WonderMart owner Perri Salka, also the lead organizer of Shop Small Greenpoint, said the shutdown means her business loses money before she even opens the door. “Tourists have a hard time navigating the shuttle bus system, and folks from other parts of the city don’t feel it’s worth the hassle, so they just wait to enjoy Greenpoint on a weekend when the train is actually running,” she said, noting that many business owners find the MTA’s outreach to be lacking at best. Shop Small Greenpoint recently surveyed 36 local small businesses and found that 91% of businesses reported a decrease in sales during shutdown weekends, with sales falling 20-24% on average. Food and beverage establishments were the hardest hit by the sales decrease. Earlier this summer, the group sent a letter to the MTA , asking for the agency to move some June shutdown weekends. The MTA rescheduled one weekend to July, perhaps paving the way for future dates to be moved as well. “The MTA cites low ridership as its reasoning, but from where shop owners stand, ridership numbers miss the point entirely. To us, December isn’t measured by how many people ride the train; it’s measured by how many people walk through our doors,” Salka continued, stating that holiday shopping brings in a quarter of her annual revenue. “Greenpoint couldn’t be closer to central Manhattan, and we’ve become, like, a mythical place, a far-off land that no one can get to because of this G train that is infamously bad,” said Natalie Como, owner of Jesus Christ Vintage. The MTA has been working since summer 2024 to complete work on an upgraded signaling system for the G train. MTA CEO Janno Lieber revealed in April that some of the delays actually stem from an entirely different issue—the tunnel under Newtown Creek requires more work than initially thought. The MTA is expected to finish work on the G train in 2027.

title.rendered: Williamsburg Gets a New Pedestrian Plaza Along the BQE
link: https://greenpointers.com/2026/08/20/williamsburg-gets-a-new-pedestrian-plaza-along-the-bqe/
date: 2026-08-20T10:51:18
content.rendered: Update August 20, 2026: An earlier version of this story attributed the quotes to a different NBK Parks Alliance staff member. Jane Jacobs would be pleased. Local elected officials and community members gathered yesterday afternoon to celebrate the opening of Jackson Street Pop-Up Plaza (1 Jackson St.), a stretch of roadway along the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway that has been transformed into a community space with furniture, planters, signage, picnic tables and moveable barriers. The project is a collaboration between the Exley, a neighborhood gay bar; the North Brooklyn Parks Alliance (NBK Parks) and the North Brooklyn Open Streets Community Coalition. It began with a simple goal: “making this corner of the neighborhood feel more welcoming,” Katie Denny Horowitz of NBK Parks told Greenpointers. The new plaza is part of the city’s Department of Transportation’s 2024 visioning process for revitalizing the BQE corridor . NBK Parks then compiled a report recommending that slip lanes be closed to create new public plazas along Meeker Avenue. In its first phase, the plaza uses relatively simple, flexible interventions to establish the street as public space and “learn how it functions before anything more permanent is contemplated,” Denny Horowitz said. The new plaza demonstrates what a more people-centered BQE corridor could look like. Photo: Greenpointers The organizations leading the project have also been working with the plaza’s neighbors as it takes shape, listening to their concerns about access and adjusting the temporary design in response. After years of community interest in reclaiming and improving the BQE corridor, Jackson Street Pop-Up Plaza has received a favorable response as another step toward turning “some of that long-term visioning into something they can actually experience today,” Denny Horowitz said. NBK Parks is to provide sanitation, care for plantings, monitor conditions and coordinate programming with its partners. This commitment responds directly to feedback from neighbors during the BQE community engagement process , who emphasized that consistent attention to cleanliness, horticulture, safety and maintenance will be essential to the new plaza’s success. Project partners will continue creating opportunities for volunteer stewardship and community programming. Photo: Greenpointers Partners leading the effort want future programming for the pedestrian plaza to build on what has already been happening organically around the Exley, a pillar of the North Brooklyn’s gay community , and this stretch of Meeker Avenue. “For several years, Pride weekend and other gatherings hosted by the Exley have demonstrated a real constituency for activating this space,” Denny Horowitz said. “Those events brought people together in an area that otherwise functioned largely as leftover roadway and helped show what it could feel like when neighbors, patrons and local businesses come together to create new pockets in the public realm.” Rather than starting from scratch, the project partners say they plan to continue working with the Exley, nearby residents and community organizations to develop programming that feels natural to the neighborhood.

content.rendered: Color is a deceptively tricky concept. What starts with red, yellow, and blue quickly morphs into infinite shades, ones that can be perceived differently from one human to the next (remember #TheDress ?). And which of those shades flatters you the most? If you’ve ever stared in bewilderment at a Sephora shelf, wondering how you’re supposed to know if you need a warm or a cool foundation, that might mean it’s time to turn to a trusted color professional. Karen Young is a long-time Greenpointer hosting color analysis sessions just across the Pulaski Bridge in Long Island City. She’s available for individual or group sessions, at-home sessions, at-home color analysis parties, corporate, retail, and brand activation events. Head to her site to book a session (they’re really fun! Happy to report that I’m a True Autumn). Check out our most recent ‘Greenpoint at Work’ profile here . Tell us a little about you, and how you ended up in this field I’ve lived in New York for about 20 years, and I would say about 16 of those years, I’ve lived in Greenpoint specifically, and I’ve had many lives here. So I have a degree in theater. I came with the intention of becoming an actor. I worked in casting for Broadway theater for a number of years. Then the recession hit in 2008-2010, [I] lost my job, worked in retail, became really fascinated and fell in love with styling, fashion, and also hiring for retail stores. So I was an HR manager, and I helped build out teams for several locations for a retailer in New York City. Through that, I became interested in recruiting in general. I’ve always had a love for advertising and marketing. So I worked in recruiting for a digital agency, and then finally recruiting in tech for a number of years. I recruited for creative marketing and design in tech. And in 2020 and 2023, I fell into some of the layoffs that are happening in that industry. And I finally said, you know what? I’m tired of being laid off. I’m tired of my path being controlled by a corporate entity, so it was time to really do some soul searching. And when I sat down to think about my values and what I really wanted out of a career, one of the things that kept coming up over and over again was color analysis. I became interested in color analysis at the age of 10, believe it or not. I discovered the Color Me Beautiful book and videos by Carole Jackson. Carole Jackson is like a pioneer in this field and really started the movement that was color analysis in the 1980s. So I was 10 years old watching these videos and reading books about color analysis, so it’s always been a hobby of mine. It came up again recently, so I decided to invest in some training. I flew to Toronto. I had no experience actually performing color analysis or draping at this time. But I flew to Toronto just basically for the plot, and I ended up falling in love with draping and with color analysis and with this whole experience. That was in 2024 and I came back to New York, and I couldn’t stop thinking about it and I knew I had to start my own business, so that’s how I formed Key Color Analysis. It does sort of feel like there is a bit of a resurgence in color analysis because I used to think of it also as a very sort of 1980s/1990s kind of thing. But now more and more people, especially younger women as well, are getting interested in it. Absolutely. So this really started picking up in 2020 when everyone was spending a lot of time at home in a pandemic, and people were working from home. All of a sudden we’re showing up on screens with just our face. And it became really important to show up right on a screen. So that’s how people became interested in color analysis, and it’s become this whole trend. For me, I’ve always been interested in it, and I’m glad to see it’s back because I think everyone should get a color analysis. And I think it serves everyone. It’s more than just a trend. It’s how you show up. Absolutely. I like to say that the butterfly has its colors for a reason and so do we. We are part of nature. So color analysis gives you a framework to show up in colors that harmonize with your natural features. It’s way more than just a quiz. It’s actually more in depth than that. It’s more complicated. I hold color analysis sessions in my studio in Long Island City, which is just a quick hop, skip and jump away from Greenpoint.  A mock-up of the “license premises” in the license agreement for the Transmitter Park project. Photo: City of New York



 Schmitt and Cohen are seasoned professionals with restaurant industry experience. The pair told Greenpointers that they met about six months ago and hit it off instantly. Cohen is a longtime North Brooklyn resident who is very involved in the local community and with organizations like North Brooklyn Angels . Alongside other partners, Cohen has ownership in Chez Ma Tante , Lilia, Saint Vitus , and Dead Letter No. 9 . Schmitt owns Dakota’s on the Cove in the Bronx and DJ’s at Bayside in Queens, both waterfront restaurants overlooking marinas, very similar to the new Transmitter Park project.

 The possible site of Transmitter Park’s new restaurant can be seen behind a member of Friends of Transmitter Park. Photo: Friends of Transmitter Park



 The timeline of the Transmitter Park project, as detailed on the current draft of the license agreement , begins with overall design.



 Alexander Han, Chief of Concessions for NYC Parks, told Greenpointers that his department is currently working with David Schmitt and his team to develop a design that is “complementary to park.”  Han said that the public will have the opportunity to review designs and provide input during a public hearing next month. The license agreement states that the second phase of the Transmitter Park project includes the demolition of the abandoned building that’s located inside the park, followed by the third phase which includes building a new structure for the restaurant with a public restroom, something that local volunteer organization Friends of Transmitter Park has been fighting for . According to the current draft of the license agreement, the restaurant will serve lunch and dinner daily from May 1 to October 31. Han explained that all of these details could change. Formal designs have not be submitted and approved. content.rendered: A few weeks ago, customers began pouring into Greenpoint staple Star Deli (176 Nassau Ave.) after a series of videos posted by Instagram creator @pov.outside unexpectedly went viral, amassing more than 4 million views. Google Reviews multiplied overnight and strangers from around the world donated more than $6,000 to repair the deli’s broken freezer. Behind the sudden surge was Shuayb Qureshi, a software engineer at Meta by trade and champion of neighborhood businesses by choice. Qureshi has quietly become Greenpoint’s very own Superman, putting on his cape with the mission of aiding struggling local businesses. His account has accumulated over 15,000 followers on Instagram with his videos reaching anywhere between 5,000 to over 1 million views. On weekends and evenings after his 9-to-5, Qureshi spends his time in the community, committed to spreading the word to his followers. What inspires him the most is his family back in Toronto. “I miss that love. When I go to see these restaurants, especially once I make a video about them, I feel so much love,” he said. “I feel like they’re my parents in a lot of ways or my family.”









 Photos: Shuayb Qureshi



 Although he lives in Manhattan, Qureshi turned to Reddit to ask Greenpoint residents which businesses need help most. Hundreds responded, making the project a collaborative effort shaped by the people who know the neighborhood best.  After visiting, he found himself lingering in neighborhood delis long after finishing lunch and chatting with owners instead of rushing home, something he rarely experienced elsewhere in the city.  “I don’t feel the soul of trendy places in Manhattan,” he said. “But I feel like when you go to places in Greenpoint, especially places like Star Deli, there’s so much soul in that.” As his friendship with Star Deli owner Zbigniew, known as “Mr. Z,” grew, Qureshi was inspired to launch the GoFundMe and ultimately credits its success to the people.  “It’s not just Americans, there’s people from Canada, people from the UK, and people from Poland donating,” he said. “It’s literally the world, and I feel so humbled because everyone participated.” It was a fitting response for a neighborhood long shaped by immigrant communities. For Qureshi, Greenpoint’s changing population is one factor behind the challenges facing longtime businesses. “With transplants, we like to go to popular places like Whole Foods and restaurants that are popping on Instagram. We’re not going to delis, so I think gentrification is an issue,” he claims. “But I do think there’s some gentrifiers, people like me who actually love family-owned places.”



 Greenpoint has spent years grappling with rising commercial rents , shifting demographics , and changing consumer habits, all of which have contributed to the slow disappearance of longtime neighborhood establishments . As more people discover restaurants through Instagram, TikTok, and Google Reviews instead of neighborhood word of mouth, many legacy establishments have had to adjust to a rapidly evolving business environment.  Qureshi believes social media can help longtime businesses connect with newer residents. Before his video, Star Deli had very few Google Reviews. Today, it has more than 300. “If you look at people like Mr. Z, he works seven days a week, and is still trying to adapt to the new people,” Qureshi said. “But I don’t think they know how to market via social media.” Handing Mr. Z the proceeds from the fundraiser was the most rewarding moment of the project. “When I gave Mr. Z the GoFundMe money, it felt so good,” he said. “To the point where I thought I don’t know what I’ve done in life to deserve to feel this good.” Qureshi’s videos have even inspired other influencers to visit Star Deli and create videos of their own. For Qureshi, that’s the goal, not to become the face of a movement, but to create momentum that continues long after he puts down his camera.  “Having this influence comes with some sort of power,” Qureshi said. “I’m getting some sort of status here, but the people who come in and donate anonymously, they’re not expecting anything, and they really truly are the real heroes here.”  



 Treats on display at Star Deli. Photo: Shuayb Qureshi



 Star Deli wasn’t the only business to benefit from his efforts. Qureshi has since turned his attention to Tony’s Pizzeria (175 Nassau Ave.), another longtime family business across from Star Deli facing increased competition from trendier spots like Chrissy’s Pizza, Diamond Slice, and Paulie Gee’s Slice Shop. Tony’s owner, Salvatore Sinnona, appreciates Qureshi’s impact.  “We’ve definitely seen new faces come in mentioning his videos or social media, and that kind of exposure is something money can’t always buy,” Sinnona said. “More importantly, it reminds people that supporting local businesses keeps our communities alive.” Sinnona says attracting first-time customers has become increasingly difficult. “The biggest challenge is competing with large chains and the convenience of online ordering,” he said. “People have endless choices, and many automatically go to big brands without realizing there are family-owned places making everything with pride and tradition.”  Qureshi insists the internet isn’t what saved Star Deli, the people did. Every Instagram view became a customer. Every Google review became a recommendation. Every anonymous donation became an act of neighborhood solidarity. “Go talk to your local business owners,” Qureshi urges. “You never know, they might become family.”

 The arrangement is through the Parks’ concessions program, which allows food service professionals to operate restaurants and cafes on Parks’ properties. In return, that revenue stream frees up Parks to finally build a public restroom in the park, a long-overdue goal for many in the neighborhood.



 And if you’re wondering, “Well, why can’t Parks just decide to build a bathroom as is?” The answer is that building public restrooms in New York City is a bit more complicated (and expensive) than it may appear.
